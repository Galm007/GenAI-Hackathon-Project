FICTIONAL DEMO ESSAY: Sam Rivera
Done by gridiii | AdCom D01 | Original fictional writing for testing

Every Thursday, I rewrite the same small grocery sign. It begins as a price, becomes a sentence, and ends as an argument with the space on a shelf. "Two for three dollars" is clear until a customer asks whether one costs a dollar fifty. The answer is yes. The sign should have said so.

I started working at our neighborhood grocery during ninth grade. At first, I thought doing the job well meant moving quickly: restock the shelf, finish the transaction, get to the next task. I also helped look after my younger sibling after school. My homework happened in short stretches, and my early transcript shows how poorly I managed those stretches. That is part of my record. So is what changed afterward.

In tenth grade, I began meeting a teacher once a week to plan assignments around my shifts. I learned to ask about a confusing problem before it became six missing ones. By junior year I was taking AP Biology and AP Statistics and could explain why a chart was misleading instead of only noticing that it looked wrong. The improvement was gradual enough that I nearly missed it. I noticed when a classmate asked me how to organize a lab notebook.

Our biology class later partnered with a community center on a small health-information project. Under the coordinator's supervision, I helped prepare plain-language handouts about the center's public events. I did not provide medical advice or work with patient records. My job was to make directions usable: where to go, what time to arrive, and whom to ask if something was unclear.

The first handout looked complete to our group. A visitor pointed out that "registration required" did not explain whether registration cost money. Another wanted to know whether children could come. I recognized the problem from the grocery sign. We had included information we possessed, rather than answering questions a person actually needed to ask. We revised the handout with the coordinator and added a contact point for anything we could not answer.

I began noticing those gaps elsewhere. A graph could show an average while hiding a wide range. An explanation could be accurate and still be inaccessible. At the store, I tested a simpler shelf label and watched which questions people still asked. At school, I became more careful about distinguishing what our small experiment showed from what we merely hoped was true.

I want to study biology while continuing to explore community health. The work that interests me asks both how a system functions and who can understand the explanation. I am still learning to balance school, paid work, and family responsibilities; I am also learning that asking a good question is a practical skill. When I rewrite the Thursday sign now, I leave a little space at the bottom. Usually, someone has one more question.
